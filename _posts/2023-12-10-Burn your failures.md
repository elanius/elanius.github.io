title:  "Burn Your Failures"
categories: [outdoor]
---
Outside, it was still relatively cold. Night temperatures remained below zero, but daytime temperatures had risen above freezing, causing the thick blanket of snow to slowly melt. I had a feeling the temperature would continue to rise in the coming days, even though it was December. It was a Sunday, and I felt the need to go outside — alone — to the woods. I wanted to clear my mind and enjoy the winter landscape while there was still snow and not just mud.

As I walked up the hill, I passed branches of bushes and small trees bent under the weight of heavy snow. Shaking them caused them to spring back to their original positions. It made me reflect on how something as delicate as a snowflake — melting instantly upon touch — can have the power to press strong branches to the ground, sometimes even breaking them. Isn’t life similar? Small, almost unnoticeable things can weigh us down, yet a simple shake — a jolt from life’s challenges — can help us rise again.

I encountered only one doe, which bolted as I pushed through an area of fallen trees. Moving quietly was impossible, and it heard me from afar. I was disappointed; I had hoped to see more wildlife. By now, I was nearly at the top of the hill, approaching the spot where I had planned to build a fire and warm myself.

I figured it wouldn’t be a big deal to start a fire. Everything was buried under a thick blanket of snow, but I had around half a matchbox of matches, and I knew I could find dry branches hanging from the trees — the ones that never touch the ground. There were plenty of them.

I scraped a small hollow in the snow, laid a few logs on the ground to keep everything off the wet, and gathered a handful of the thinnest twigs for kindling. I struck a match. A couple of twigs caught, flickered nicely for a few seconds, then died. I tried again. Same story, three or four times. The twigs weren’t as dry as I’d convinced myself. Clearly this wasn’t going to work. I flattened everything and went looking for something better.

Next I tried bark. Some of the branches had thin, papery strips peeling off — almost like natural tinder. I collected a good handful, piled it on the base, and lit another match. Same disappointment: it burned cheerfully while the match was alive, but the moment the match went out, the flame gave up. I even tried blowing gently to give it more oxygen. Nothing.

Now I understood it wasn’t going to be easy, but I still didn’t doubt I’d get there. I just needed a proper starter. So I took my knife and shaved fine curls from the driest-looking branches I could find. I flattened the old pile again, made a neat little nest of shavings, and struck another match. Same result — a brief hopeful flare and then nothing. I kept blowing, added the tiniest sticks, and lit yet another match. The wood was simply too cold and damp. The temperature was hovering right around zero, the air heavy with moisture. Had it stayed properly below freezing, all that humidity would have turned to frost and maybe something would have been truly dry.

That’s when the thought crept in: I might actually fail. My fingers were cold, my boots were wet, and I really wanted that fire to warm myself. But worse than the cold was the bruise my ego was about to take.

I opened the matchbox. Four left. Four chances. Okay, now I had to do it properly.

I flattened the latest sad pile with my hand and started over, very carefully this time. I built a tight little teepee of the driest twigs I had, left a small tunnel on one side so I could slide the match right underneath. I lit it, held the flame steady, and prayed. The same pattern started again — tiny flames that couldn’t grow. But then I noticed something: even after the flame died, a few embers glowed. When I blew on them, they brightened and made new embers. More were born than died.

I kept blowing. I changed the direction of the heat — instead of letting it rise and escape, my breath pushed it down and sideways into the pile. Suddenly all my previous failed attempts — the twigs, the bark, the shavings — became fuel. They fed the embers. The glowing patch grew and grew until there was enough heat to dry the surrounding wood and finally produce real flames. When I stopped blowing, the fire switched back to its natural upward flow, but now the flames were strong enough to eat fresh sticks. I started feeding it carefully, and within a minute I had a proper little blaze going.

It was completely unplanned. And then it hit me: if I hadn’t had all those failed piles lying there, I wouldn’t have had enough material to build that bed of embers. I was literally burning my earlier failures to reach success. Without them, I would have stayed cold.

It sounds like one of those motivational stories about the frog churning milk into butter, but this one was mine, and it landed hard. **Sometimes failure isn’t the opposite of success — it’s the fuel.**

Later I built the fire up, warmed my hands, and — something I literally never do — took a selfie, just so I’d remember exactly how that moment felt.
